Backups and disaster recovery: what survives when the WAL itself doesn't

Lesson 86 named the write-ahead log as the thing durability is actually built on: a crash loses nothing, because recovery replays the log back to exactly where COMMIT left off. Lesson 85 named the read replica: another instance, replaying the same log, that survives one machine dying outright. Neither survives a bad DELETE FROM orders with no WHERE clause, or a disk that corrupts silently, or a region that disappears — because a replica faithfully replays mistakes too. That's what backups are for, and the two numbers every team should be able to state before trusting one.

1 · A replica protects against the wrong kind of failure

Lesson 85's read replica exists to survive hardware dying: the primary's disk fails, its process crashes and won't come back, the machine itself is gone — the replica is already caught up (or close to it) and can be promoted to take over. But a replica is built to do one thing faithfully: replay every change the primary made, in order, forever. A mistaken UPDATE that sets every row's price to zero is not a hardware failure — it's a correct, successfully committed change that happens to be wrong. The replica replays it exactly like any other committed change, within milliseconds, and now both copies are wrong. Replication answers "what if a machine dies." It has no answer at all for "what if the data itself was bad the moment it was written."

Different failure, different tool: a replica is a second copy of the current state, kept current on purpose. A backup is a copy of some past state, kept deliberately behind, precisely so a mistake that has already reached every live, current copy can still be undone by stepping backward to before it happened.

2 · Two numbers, decided before anything goes wrong

"We have backups" is not a plan by itself — it hides two separate questions that need separate numbers attached, decided in advance, not improvised during an incident. The first: if disaster strikes right now, how much data can the business accept losing — five minutes of orders, one hour, one day? That ceiling is the RPO, Recovery Point Objective — how far back in time the most recent usable recovery point is allowed to be. The second question is about time, not data: once the team starts recovering, how long can the service stay down before that outage itself becomes unacceptable? That ceiling is the RTO, Recovery Time Objective.

These two numbers drive everything about what backup strategy is even worth building. A one-day RPO is satisfied by a nightly full dump. A five-minute RPO is not — nothing that only runs once a night can promise five minutes, no matter how good the restore process is. An RTO measured in minutes rules out a restore process that takes six hours to even finish copying files, regardless of how little data it loses. Both numbers are business decisions, not engineering ones — "how much pain can we tolerate" — but they are the only honest way to then go choose, and defend, a specific backup mechanism.

3 · The mechanisms, from coarsest to finest-grained

A full backup — a complete copy of every table, taken at one moment — is the simplest mechanism and the easiest to reason about: restoring it means loading that one file back in. Its weakness is exactly its RPO: taken once a night, it can lose up to nearly 24 hours of writes, and taking it is itself expensive enough that most teams can't run it continuously. An incremental backup narrows that gap by only copying what changed since the last backup, which is cheaper to take and store — but a restore now means replaying a full backup plus a chain of increments in order, and that chain being unbroken matters: lose or corrupt one increment in the middle, and everything after it in the chain is unusable too.

The finest-grained mechanism is one this course already taught the raw ingredient for, without naming what it enables: Lesson 86's write-ahead log. Postgres can continuously archive every WAL segment it generates, off to separate storage, as it's written — not once a night, but constantly. Combined with a periodic full backup as a base, that continuous WAL archive lets a restore replay forward to any moment in between, not just to whenever the last nightly dump happened to run. That capability is point-in-time recovery (PITR): restore the base backup, then replay the archived WAL forward and stop exactly one second before the mistaken DELETE committed, rather than losing everything back to last night's dump.

Why PITR is the WAL paying rent twice: Lesson 86 built the WAL to make a local crash recoverable — replay since the last checkpoint, nothing lost. Lesson 87 showed the same stream read externally as a row-level change feed for CDC. PITR is a third job for the identical artifact: archived for the long term instead of recycled after a checkpoint, it becomes the fine-grained time axis a full backup alone doesn't have. One mechanism, three different consumers of the same sequential log.

4 · Lining the mechanisms up against the two numbers

Fits an RTO measured in minutesonly if the dataset is small enough to reload fastusually, if the increment chain is shortyes, for the data side — but restoring and replaying still takes real time

None of these three substitute for Lesson 85's replica. A replica's whole job is staying current, seconds behind, so a hardware failure costs almost no time at all — exactly what an RTO measured in minutes actually needs for that failure mode. A backup's job is staying deliberately behind, so a logical mistake that's already been faithfully replicated everywhere can still be undone. Production systems that take both seriously run both at once, for two different failure modes, not one in place of the other.

Q: "Walk me through your backup strategy." A strong answer, out loud: "First I'd ask what RPO and RTO the business actually needs — how much data loss is tolerable, and how long can we be down — because those two numbers decide which mechanism is even worth building, not the other way around. A nightly full backup is simplest but caps RPO near 24 hours. If we need tighter than that, I'd add continuous WAL archiving on top of periodic full backups, which gives point-in-time recovery — restore to any timestamp, not just to last night's dump. That's a different failure mode than replication, though: a read replica protects against a machine dying, because it's a current copy kept current on purpose. A backup protects against a mistake or corruption that's already been faithfully replicated to every live copy, including the replica — a bad DELETE replicates perfectly. I'd want both running, and I'd want the RTO side tested, not just assumed: restoring a backup you've never actually practiced restoring is a plan on paper, not a plan." The signal: naming RPO/RTO as the thing that should be decided first, and correctly separating what a backup protects against from what a replica protects against, instead of treating "we have backups" as a complete answer.

Scope line

MISSION.md excludes NoSQL, sharding, and distributed systems beyond vocabulary level, and a full production disaster-recovery plan drifts well past that line fast: multi-region failover, backup encryption and retention policy, automated restore testing pipelines, cloud-provider-specific snapshot tooling. None of that is this lesson's job. The point here is the vocabulary and the mental model — RPO and RTO as the two numbers that should be decided before a mechanism is chosen, and the backup-vs-replica distinction, since "we have backups" and "we have a replica" answer two different disaster questions that get conflated constantly in real conversations.

Series.skew() and .kurt(): shape statistics, and the min-count NaN trap

Lesson 137's own scope line named .sem() and .skew()/.kurt() as the nearest open statistics gaps but left them for a future round's own fresh scan to confirm still open, per this course's "fresh scan each round" convention. Today's own fresh scan re-confirmed it directly: grepped every lesson body and every glossary row case-insensitively for .sem(, .skew(, .kurt(, np.skew(, and np.kurtosis( — the only hits were two prior "skewed data" mentions in plain English (Lesson 3's fill-value reasoning, Lesson 131's log-transform byline), never the actual methods; zero hits anywhere for .sem(), .kurt(), or either NumPy spelling. Picked .skew()/.kurt() as today's pair — a closely related higher-moment shape-statistics duo, same family as Lesson 137's .std()/.var() but describing a distribution's asymmetry and tail-weight rather than its spread — and left .sem() (a simple .std()-derived ratio, confirmed below) as the next nearest gap, since building out all three in one lesson would blur the two genuinely distinct traps this lesson confirms.

1 · skew() measures asymmetry; its sign tells you which tail is long

Confirmed directly (pandas 3.1.1, numpy 2.5.5) on three shapes of the same rough size:

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

symmetric   = pd.Series([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
right_tail  = pd.Series([1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 100])  # one huge outlier, high end
left_tail   = pd.Series([1, 97, 98, 98, 98, 99, 99, 99, 100])  # one huge outlier, low end

symmetric.skew()   # 0.0                 -- no asymmetry
right_tail.skew()  # 2.9948337189548666  -- positive: long tail stretches RIGHT (toward +inf)
left_tail.skew()   # -2.9958956056874224 -- negative: long tail stretches LEFT (toward -inf)

Series.skew() reports a single number: 0 means symmetric, a positive number means the long tail points toward larger values (one huge high outlier, like the income-distribution shape Lesson 3's median-fill reasoning already assumed without naming the number), and a negative number means the long tail points toward smaller values. The sign names where the tail stretches, not where most of the rows actually sit.

2 · kurt() is EXCESS kurtosis — a normal distribution scores ~0, not ~3

Confirmed directly on a 100,000-value random normal sample:

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
normal_sample = pd.Series(rng.normal(size=100_000))

normal_sample.skew()  # ~0.007  -- a true normal distribution has 0 skew
normal_sample.kurt()  # ~0.023  -- a true normal distribution ALSO scores ~0 here, not ~3

right_tail.kurt()     # 8.977116427636945  -- heavy-tailed / sharply peaked relative to normal

Series.kurt() reports excess kurtosis — confirmed directly, a near-perfect normal distribution scores close to 0 here, not the raw-kurtosis value of 3 that a statistics textbook's formula produces before subtracting 3 for the normal baseline. The trap: quoting "kurtosis of 3 means normal" in an interview is the textbook's raw formula, but pandas has already subtracted that 3 for you — treating a pandas .kurt() reading of 3 as "normal" would be off by the entire baseline.

Confirmed directly, the minimum row count each needs before it stops returning NaN: .skew() needs at least 3 non-null values — a Series of length 1 or 2 returns a silent NaN, and length exactly 3 is the first point it returns a real number (0.0 for three evenly-spaced values). .kurt() needs at least 4 — lengths 1 through 3 all return NaN, and length 4 is the first real number. Both also return NaN on a constant column (every value identical, zero variance) regardless of length, since both formulas divide by a power of the standard deviation and a constant column's standard deviation is exactly 0. No exception, no warning, either way — the same "no crash, quietly wrong" family as Lesson 137's single-row .std() trap, describe() (Lesson 49), and reindex() (Lesson 47), but with a higher row-count threshold than .std()/.var()'s "just one row" trigger.

3 · pandas-only: no np.skew() or np.kurtosis() to fall back on

Confirmed directly, unlike Lesson 137's .std()/.var() (which exist on both sides with different ddof defaults), NumPy has no direct equivalent at all:

hasattr(np, "skew")      # False
hasattr(np, "kurtosis")  # False

There is no cross-library default-mismatch trap here to watch for — only the pandas method exists, so the only confirmed traps are the excess-kurtosis baseline (Section 2) and the minimum-row-count NaN (the callout above). A DataFrame.skew()/.kurt() call runs column-by-column exactly like .std()/.var() do, returning NaN for any column that is constant or too short, independently of its neighbors.

Q: "What does a skew() value tell you, and what's the gotcha with kurt()?" A strong answer, out loud: "skew() measures asymmetry — zero is symmetric, positive means the long tail stretches toward larger values, negative means it stretches toward smaller values, so the sign tells me which direction the outliers are pulling. kurt() measures tail weight relative to a normal distribution, but pandas reports excess kurtosis, so a true normal distribution scores around 0, not the 3 you'd get from the raw textbook formula — I'd be careful not to compare a pandas kurt() reading against the raw-3 baseline by mistake. Both also need a minimum row count to return a real number at all — skew() needs at least 3 values, kurt() needs at least 4 — and both silently return NaN below that or on a constant column, with no error to flag it." The signal: naming the excess-kurtosis baseline unprompted, since quoting "3 means normal" against a pandas kurt() output is the single most common mistake this topic invites.

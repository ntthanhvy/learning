describe(): the fast summary-stats sanity check

Lesson 48's own closing teaser named this directly as the strongest next candidate: the single most interview-common pandas method still totally uncovered by this course. Lesson 2 already taught .info()/.dtypes/.head() as the first-look trio when a file lands on your desk — describe() is the missing fourth move: one call answering "what do the actual VALUES look like."

Recap, in one line

Lesson 2's .info() tells you the shape and dtypes; Lesson 2's .head() shows a handful of raw rows. Neither tells you whether amount ranges from 10 to 100 or from -500 to a million. describe() is the one call that answers that — a full five-number-plus summary of every numeric column, computed in one line instead of five separate .mean()/.std()/.min()/.max() calls.

1 · describe() on a numeric column: the SQL GROUP BY-free summary

The real clean 4-row orders_raw.csv slice Lessons 6-48 have used:

clean
--   order_id customer  amount order_date
-- 0        1       An   120.0 2026-01-05
-- 1        6       An    42.0 2026-01-10
-- 2        2     Binh    35.5 2026-01-06
-- 3        5     Binh   180.0 2026-01-09

describe() on the amount column returns all of this at once:

clean["amount"].describe()
-- count      4.000000
-- mean      94.375000
-- std       68.793623
-- min       35.500000
-- 25%       40.375000
-- 50%       81.000000
-- 75%      135.000000
-- max      180.000000
-- Name: amount, dtype: float64

The 50% row is the median — the exact same "sorted-order middle value" concept Lesson 3 introduced as a safer fill value than the mean, just appearing here as one row of the table instead of a standalone call. The SQL bridge: this is what would otherwise be a single query stacking COUNT, AVG, STDDEV, MIN, MAX, and PERCENTILE_CONT(0.25/0.5/0.75) side by side — describe() is that whole query pre-written.

2 · The gotcha: describe() on a str column silently changes shape

Here's the trap this lesson leads with. Loading orders_raw.csv raw, before any pd.to_numeric() coercion (Lesson 3), the literal text value "unknown" in row 2 blocks pandas from auto-detecting amount as numeric — it loads as a plain text (str) column:

raw = pd.read_csv("orders_raw.csv")
raw["amount"].dtype              # str -- NOT float64, "unknown" blocked it
raw["amount"].describe()
-- count         6
-- unique        6
-- top       120.0
-- freq          1
-- Name: amount, dtype: object

No error. No warning. describe() silently swaps its ENTIRE output shape: instead of mean/std/percentiles, a text column gets count/unique/top/freq — a categorical summary, treating every distinct string (including the numeric-looking ones) as just another label. This is Lesson 2's own dtype-checking discipline paying off directly: describe() is not a dtype-agnostic magic box, it's dtype-aware, and it never tells you which mode it used — you have to already know the column's dtype going in, exactly why Lesson 2 taught checking .dtypes/.info() first. Join the "no crash, quietly wrong" family already flagged for reindex() (Lesson 47) and str.extract() (Lesson 42): the output still LOOKS like a normal describe() table, just answering an entirely different question than the one you probably meant to ask.

3 · include="all": widening past "numeric columns only"

Plain DataFrame.describe() (no column selected) defaults to numeric columns ONLY — any text column, like a clean, correctly-typed customer column, is silently dropped from the result, not an error:

clean.describe()
-- (only order_id and amount columns appear -- customer and order_date silently excluded
--  from the numeric-only default; order_date is its own special case, shown next)

include="all" widens it to summarize every column regardless of dtype — numeric columns still get their mean/std/percentiles, and non-numeric columns like customer get unique/top/freq in the SAME table, with NaN filling whichever cells don't apply to a given column's dtype:

clean.describe(include="all")
--         order_id customer      amount           order_date
-- count   4.000000        4    4.000000                    4
-- unique       NaN        2         NaN                  NaN
-- top          NaN       An         NaN                  NaN
-- freq         NaN        2         NaN                  NaN
-- mean    3.500000      NaN   94.375000  2026-01-07 12:00:00
-- ...

Note order_date, a real datetime64 column: it gets a mean (the average timestamp) and percentiles, but no std — pandas computes a "typical" datetime differently than a typical duration, a genuinely different summary shape than either the numeric or the pure-text case.

4 · describe() after groupby(): one block per group

describe() chains after groupby() exactly the way agg() does (Lesson 4) — instead of one describe() table for the whole column, you get one full row of stats per group:

clean.groupby("customer")["amount"].describe()
--           count    mean         std   min     25%     50%      75%    max
-- customer
-- An          2.0   81.00   55.154329  42.0  61.500   81.00  100.500  120.0
-- Binh        2.0  107.75  102.176930  35.5  71.625  107.75  143.875  180.0

This is the fastest way to eyeball "does every customer's spending look reasonable" in one call, rather than a separate .agg(["count","mean","std","min","max"]) spelled out by hand — and it's the natural first move before reaching for Lesson 16's transform() or Lesson 30's per-row broadcasting once something in the summary looks off.

Q: "A teammate hands you a new CSV. What's the very first pandas call you run, and why?" A strong answer, out loud: "info() and dtypes first, to confirm every column parsed as the type I expect — then describe() immediately after, since it's the fastest sanity check on the actual VALUES: are there negative amounts that shouldn't exist, is the max absurdly large, does the count match the row count I expect. The trap I watch for is describe() on a column that LOOKS numeric but loaded as text — a single bad string like 'unknown' silently flips describe()'s whole output from mean/std/percentiles to count/unique/top/freq, no error at all, so the summary table itself can look completely normal while quietly answering the wrong question. That's exactly why I check dtypes before I trust a describe() table." The signal: naming describe() as dtype-aware, not dtype-agnostic, and tying it back to the load-and-inspect habit rather than treating it as a standalone party trick.

5 · The SQL bridge

describe() on a numeric column is a single query stacking COUNT(col), AVG(col), STDDEV(col), MIN(col), MAX(col), and PERCENTILE_CONT(0.25/0.5/0.75) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY col) as separate output columns of one row — SQL has no single built-in keyword that produces all of it at once. describe() on a text column is closer to SELECT COUNT(*), COUNT(DISTINCT col), MODE() WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY col) plus that mode's own frequency — a genuinely different query, which is exactly why pandas silently switches shape depending on dtype: it's really running one of two different summary queries under one shared method name.

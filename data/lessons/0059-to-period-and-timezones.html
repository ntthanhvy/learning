.dt.to_period() and timezones: calendar buckets, not fixed grids

Lesson 32 pulled parts out of a datetime column with the .dt accessor; Lesson 38's resample() built a fixed-frequency grid (every day, every week) with gaps filled in. Today closes two things neither one covers: labeling each row with the CALENDAR bucket it belongs to ("this is a January row", not just "here's a timestamp"), and what happens the moment a timestamp needs a timezone attached.

1 · to_period(): a calendar label, not a fixed grid

.dt.to_period(freq) converts each row's exact Timestamp into a Period — the calendar bucket that timestamp falls in, printed like 2026-01 for a month. Group by it directly, no resample() grid required:

clean["order_date"].dt.to_period("M")
-- every 2026-01-xx date becomes the same Period, "2026-01"

clean.groupby(clean["order_date"].dt.to_period("M"))["amount"].sum()
-- 2026-01    377.5   (all 4 clean orders fall in one month here)

Confirmed directly against the real orders_raw.csv clean 4-row slice (An/Binh, 01-05 through 01-10): grouping by to_period("M") gives one row, 2026-01 → 377.5 — the exact same number as resample("ME") on the same data — but the RESULT differs in a way that matters: to_period()'s index is a calendar label (2026-01), while resample()'s index is a Timestamp naming the period's END (2026-01-31). Same total, different index dtype and meaning.

2 · Why not just always resample()?

Lesson 38 already named resample()'s whole point: build a FIXED, gap-included grid — every day or week gets a row even with zero orders. to_period() does the opposite job — it only labels rows that actually exist, with no grid-building step and no empty buckets manufactured. Reach for to_period() when the question is "which month does this row belong to" (a label, feeding straight into a plain groupby()); reach for resample() when the question is "show me every period in range, including the empty ones."

p = clean["order_date"].dt.to_period("M")
p + 1
-- 2026-02 for every row -- Period arithmetic shifts by whole periods, "1 month forward"

p.dt.to_timestamp()
-- back to a real Timestamp -- 2026-01-01, the period's START by default

A genuinely surprising finding, confirmed directly rather than assumed: a Period does not compare equal to the plain string that prints the same way. pd.Period("2026-01", freq="M") == "2026-01" is False — pandas does not parse the right-hand string into a Period for the comparison. Compare against another real Period (pd.Period("2026-01")) instead, or convert first.

3 · Timezones: tz_localize() attaches, tz_convert() shifts

A plain parsed datetime column (like order_date here) is tz-naive: pandas has no idea what timezone that clock time is measured in. Two methods handle timezones, and they are not interchangeable:

clean["order_date"].dt.tz_localize("UTC")
-- ATTACHES a timezone label -- clock numbers stay 2026-01-05 00:00:00, now tagged +00:00
-- dtype becomes datetime64[us, UTC]

localized.dt.tz_convert("Asia/Ho_Chi_Minh")
-- SHIFTS the clock to the same real instant in another zone -- 2026-01-05 07:00:00+07:00

tz_localize() only stamps a label on — the clock digits (00:00:00) never move. tz_convert() requires the value to already be tz-aware and shifts the actual clock digits to show the same real-world instant in the new zone. Confirmed directly, both raise (not silently do the wrong thing):

localized.dt.tz_localize("UTC")
-- TypeError: Already tz-aware, use tz_convert to convert

clean["order_date"].dt.tz_convert("UTC")
-- TypeError: Cannot convert tz-naive timestamps, use tz_localize to localize

clean["order_date"] > localized
-- TypeError: Invalid comparison between dtype=datetime64[us] and DatetimeArray
-- pandas refuses to compare naive against aware rather than silently guessing a zone

The rule of thumb: tz_localize() exactly once, right after loading, to say what timezone the source data actually means — then tz_convert() any time a different zone needs to be displayed or compared.

4 · SQL bridge

to_period() is the pandas equivalent of grouping by DATE_TRUNC('month', order_date) — a calendar-bucket label used only for rows that exist, not a manufactured empty-inclusive grid (that's resample(), the closer cousin of a generated calendar table joined with LEFT JOIN). tz_localize()/tz_convert() map to Postgres's AT TIME ZONE: applying it to a plain timestamp column attaches a zone (like tz_localize); applying it to a timestamptz column re-renders it in another zone (like tz_convert) — same naive-vs-aware distinction, same two different operations under one SQL-looking clause.

5 · Why this earns its keep in an interview

"Group sales by month" sounds trivial until the interviewer adds "...but only for months that actually had orders" (to_period() + groupby()) versus "...and show every month in the range, zeros included" (resample(), Lesson 38) — naming which one fits, unprompted, is the signal. Timezones show up the moment "the data comes from a different region's servers" gets mentioned — knowing that pandas refuses to silently compare naive against aware, and which of the two methods to reach for, heads off a whole class of silent off-by-some-hours bugs before they ship.

Q: "Our orders table has a plain datetime column, but the analytics team just told you it's actually recorded in UTC and needs to display in the user's local time zone. What do you do, and what could go wrong?" A strong answer, out loud: "First I'd tz_localize('UTC') on the column — that just stamps the UTC label on without moving any clock digits, since the values are already correct UTC times. Then tz_convert() to the target zone for display, which actually shifts the numbers to the same real instant elsewhere. The failure mode to watch for: calling tz_localize a second time, or on data that's actually already local time, silently mislabels every row — and pandas will then refuse any comparison against a naive column, which is actually the safety net that catches the mistake early instead of shipping wrong hours." The signal: naming the naive-vs-aware distinction correctly and knowing localize attaches while convert shifts.

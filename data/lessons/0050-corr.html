corr(): does this actually move with that?

Lesson 49's own closing teaser named this batch again: .corr(), convert_dtypes(), .xs(), and droplevel() have sat uncovered since Lesson 47 first flagged them. Of the four, .corr() is the sharpest pick — it's one of the most common "walk me through this" interview questions once a table is clean: not just summarizing one column (Lesson 49's describe()), but asking whether two columns move together at all.

Recap, in one line

describe() (Lesson 49) summarizes ONE column at a time — its own mean, spread, range. corr() is the natural next question: does column A move together with column B — and by how much?

1 · corr() between two Series: a single number from -1 to 1

A small inline table today — five sales reps, their call volume, and how many deals each closed (not touching the shared orders_raw.csv fixture, same precedent as Lesson 19's tagged-orders table and Lesson 11's inline "double-submitted export" table):

demo = pd.DataFrame({
    "rep":             ["Mai", "Long", "Chi", "Huy", "Trang"],
    "calls_made":      [10, 25, 15, 40, 30],
    "deals_closed":    [2, 6, 3, 9, 7],
    "office_size_sqm": [12, 30, 15, 8, 45],
})

One Series against another:

demo["calls_made"].corr(demo["deals_closed"])
-- 0.9958962509386671

Close to 1.0 means the two columns rise and fall together almost in lockstep — more calls, more deals, nearly every time in this data. A result near 0 means no consistent linear relationship at all; near -1 means they move in opposite directions (one rises as the other falls). corr() with itself is always 1.0 exactly (a column is perfectly, linearly related to itself) — a quick sanity check when a real result looks suspiciously high.

2 · The gotcha: a real number even between two things that shouldn't relate

The trap: corr() never tells you a number is meaningless — it just computes one, every time, whether or not the relationship makes any real-world sense:

demo["calls_made"].corr(demo["office_size_sqm"])
-- 0.17095081120783429

A rep's office floor space has no real reason to track how many calls they make — and the number reflects that (close to 0, weak). But it's not exactly 0, and with only 5 rows a stronger-looking coincidental number is easy to get by chance. This is the core interview trap with correlation: a strong corr() number is a hint to go investigate, never proof of a real relationship — the well-known "correlation is not causation" line has a concrete pandas face: the method computes the same linear-association number for two columns that are causally related, two that are coincidentally related, and two that are related only because a third factor drives them both. corr() can't distinguish any of those cases; only domain reasoning can.

A second, sharper version of the same "no error, just a number" gotcha shows up on the real orders_raw.csv fixture — order_id against amount on the clean 4-row slice Lessons 6-49 have used:

clean["amount"].corr(clean["order_id"])
-- 0.04427167913261477

order_id is an arbitrary row label with no real-world meaning — correlating anything against it is a nonsense question — yet corr() answers it anyway, silently, with a small real float instead of refusing. Nothing about the syntax or the output shape warns that the question itself doesn't make sense; the same "no crash, quietly wrong" family already flagged for describe() (Lesson 49), reindex() (Lesson 47), and str.extract() (Lesson 42) — except here it's not a wrong dtype tripping the trap, it's asking corr() a question it was never able to say no to.

3 · Whole-DataFrame corr(): a full matrix, and a real crash to know about

Calling corr() on the whole demo DataFrame (no column selected) computes every pairwise combination at once — a correlation MATRIX, symmetric across its diagonal of 1.0s:

demo[["calls_made", "deals_closed", "office_size_sqm"]].corr()
--                  calls_made  deals_closed  office_size_sqm
-- calls_made         1.000000      0.995896         0.170951
-- deals_closed       0.995896      1.000000         0.238001
-- office_size_sqm    0.170951      0.238001         1.000000

Unlike describe(), which silently changes shape rather than erroring on a non-numeric column, whole-DataFrame corr() genuinely raises when a non-numeric column is present and not excluded:

clean.corr()
-- ValueError: could not convert string to float: 'An'

clean still carries the text customer column and the datetime order_date column — neither has a linear-correlation number, and current pandas refuses to guess. numeric_only=True is the fix, narrowing to numeric columns only before computing, the same instinct as Lesson 25's select_dtypes():

clean.corr(numeric_only=True)
--           order_id    amount
-- order_id  1.000000  0.044272
-- amount    0.044272  1.000000

4 · Missing values: pairwise, not all-or-nothing

A NaN in one column doesn't disqualify the whole comparison — corr() drops only the ROW where either of the two compared columns is missing, then computes on whatever rows remain (pandas calls this pairwise deletion):

# demo with row 2's deals_closed set to NaN
demo["calls_made"].corr(demo["deals_closed"])
-- 0.9964037900472442   -- computed from the 4 remaining complete rows

That matches calling .dropna() on just those two columns first and then correlating the leftover 4 rows by hand — confirming corr()'s default NaN handling is exactly pairwise dropna, not a silent zero-fill and not a full-table dropna that would also throw away rows where other, unrelated columns are missing.

5 · The SQL bridge

corr() is SQL's own CORR(x, y) aggregate function (or CORR(x, y) OVER (PARTITION BY ...) as a window), standard in Postgres and most warehouses — same Pearson linear-correlation definition, same -1-to-1 scale, same requirement that both inputs are numeric. The whole-DataFrame matrix version has no single-query SQL equivalent — it's really N² separate CORR(colA, colB) calls, one per column pair, which is exactly why corr() as a DataFrame method (compute every pair at once) is a genuine convenience pandas offers over hand-writing that in SQL.

Q: "You run corr() and get 0.93 between marketing spend and revenue. What do you tell the team?" A strong answer, out loud: "That corr() only measures LINEAR association between those two columns — it's a real signal worth investigating, not proof spend caused the revenue. It could be reversed (more expected revenue leads to more approved spend), or a third factor could drive both (a seasonal launch pushes both up together), or with a small sample it could just be coincidence — corr() computes the same kind of number in all of those cases, it can't tell them apart. I'd treat 0.93 as a reason to dig into the mechanism, not as a conclusion by itself — same instinct as double-checking a column's actual dtype before trusting describe(), corr() needs the same 'what question did I actually just ask' check before trusting the number." The signal: naming linear-only, non-causal, and sample-size caveats without being asked, not just repeating "correlation isn't causation" as a slogan.
